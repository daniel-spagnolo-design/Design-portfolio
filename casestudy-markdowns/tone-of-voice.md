# Tone of voice

A reference guide for writing consistently across my portfolio, blog posts, and professional communications.

---

## Who I'm writing for

**Recruiters and hiring managers** looking for evidence of thinking, not just outputs. They want to understand how I work, not just what shipped.

**Design peers** who want the honest version of how projects actually unfolded. They can spot vague process-speak from a mile away.

---

## Core voice qualities

### Honest before impressive

Write about what actually happened, including the tradeoffs, mistakes, and moments of doubt. When a business need overruled a UX instinct, say so and explain why. When a project revealed a blind spot, name it. Credibility comes from transparency, not polish.

> *"This was a mistake, a clearer picture would have emerged of the different address picker scenarios with a proper investigation."*

### Human before process

Problems look business or technical on the surface. The writing reframes them as human ones. The automation project at Babylon was really about people registering without friction. The queuing project was really about giving fans a sense of control during a stressful wait. Lead with what is at stake for people.

### Specific, not general

Numbers, names, quotes, outcomes. "Almost 51% of employees never completed account creation" says more than "many users dropped off." Real quotes from research participants are more persuasive than paraphrased claims. Named references (specific researchers, papers, studies) carry more weight than "research shows".

### Direct without being blunt

Short sentences carry weight. Longer ones add context. Mix them. Get to the point before elaborating. Don't bury the finding in qualifications.

### Collaborative by default

Credit the team. Name people. Design work happens with others, and writing should reflect that. "Working with Nick, our content designer" is more honest than "the copy was refined."

### Curious and connected

Link ideas to a broader context. A button contrast problem connects to 250 million people with visual impairments. A queuing experience connects to the psychology of passive versus active time. Draw on research, real people, and other disciplines to make the point land.

## Techniques that appear naturally

**Parenthetical asides** for personality or self-awareness. These keep the tone grounded and human, and signal that the writer knows they're in a conversation.
> *(Okay I've sneakily made this two points, but I think they go together well.)*
> *(Not to pick on only one company here, but...)*

**Rhetorical questions** to guide the reader's thinking and frame what's coming next.
> *"What's missing from our perspective of who our users are, and what can go wrong for them, when we're designing products?"*

**Scene-setting openings** for longer pieces. Drop the reader into a moment before pulling back to explain what it means.
> *"Let me take you back to where it all started..."*

**Word of warning** flags. A clear, honest signal before sharing a caveat or qualification. More direct than hedging through the whole sentence.

---

## Sentence and structure rules

- No em dashes or en dashes. Use a full stop, a comma, or restructure the sentence.
- No "if X, then X" sentence constructions.
- Vary sentence length. Short sentences create rhythm. Longer ones explain.
- Use the first person. Own your decisions and your reasoning.
- Subheadings should do useful work: describe, tease, or ask a question. Avoid generic headers like "Overview" or "Summary."
- Paragraphs rarely exceed four sentences.
- Use bullet points for lists of three or more related items, not for every thought.

---

## Words and phrases to avoid

These either read as AI-generated content or are overused to the point of meaning nothing.

**Sounds like an LLM:** delve, leverage, realm, tapestry, nuance, comprehensive, vital, pivotal, showcase, underscore, vibrant, cutting-edge, foster, enhance, align with, bolster, emphasise, highlight (as a verb for explaining a point)

**UX filler words:** seamless, intuitive, frictionless, pain points (unless quoting research directly), best practice (unless citing a specific one), user-centric, human-centred (as an empty adjective with no supporting substance)

**Structural tells:** "Furthermore", "Moreover", "Additionally", "In conclusion", "It is worth noting", "At the end of the day", "In today's world"

---

## Tone by context

**Case studies:** Analytical and reflective. Show the thinking behind decisions, including what did not work. Outcomes matter, but so does the reasoning that got there. End with what the project taught you, not just what it achieved.

**Blog posts:** More conversational and opinionated. Take a clear position early. Use research, examples, and named references to back it. Practical tools and techniques are welcome. Light humour lands well here.

**Short-form writing (bios, project summaries):** Lead with what the work was and where it happened. Drop the adjectives. Let the outcomes speak.

---

## Quick reference

**Avoid this:**
> "Leveraging a human-centred approach, I was able to enhance the onboarding experience and drive meaningful outcomes across platforms."

**Write this:**
> "The registration flow was losing half its users before they'd even created an account. We needed to understand why before we changed anything."

---

## What this voice is not

Not overly academic or heavy with design jargon. Not self-promotional in a way that reads hollow. Not abstract. Not passive. Not performatively modest, and not boastful either.

The goal is writing that sounds like a thoughtful designer who has actually done the work and is willing to talk honestly about how it went.
